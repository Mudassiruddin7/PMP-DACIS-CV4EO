\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Channel pruning.}
Most pipelines start from one of three scores: the $\ell_1$ norm of a filter~\cite{li2017pruning}, the BatchNorm scale learned under a sparsity penalty~\cite{liu2017slimming}, or a first-order Taylor estimate of the loss change~\cite{molchanov2017pruning,molchanov2019importance}. He et al.~\cite{he2017channel} choose channels that best reconstruct the next layer's output and, for residual networks, prune only the inner convolutions of each block, which is the scheme we use. Discrimination-aware pruning~\cite{zhuang2018dcp} adds auxiliary classification losses to intermediate layers and keeps the channels that preserve them. DACIS asks a similar question with a cheaper statistic, the Fisher ratio of the pooled activation, and no extra training. These methods are evaluated on the classes they were trained for. Two earlier results make that evaluation look weaker than it is. Mittal et al.~\cite{mittal2018recovering} showed that removing $25$ to $50\%$ of the filters of VGG-16 and ResNet-50 at random and then fine-tuning matches ranked pruning, so that recovery, not the ranking, carries the result. Liu et al.~\cite{liu2019rethinking} found that a fine-tuned pruned network is no better than the same architecture trained from scratch. We ask the same question when the classes at test time differ from those used to choose the channels. MetaPruning~\cite{liu2019metapruning} also uses the word ``meta'', but there a hypernetwork predicts weights for candidate architectures; our meta stage is ordinary episodic training whose gradients feed a later pruning step.

\paragraph{Is the ranking needed?}
Random or trivial baselines have been strong in several pruning settings. Besides the two studies above, the lottery-ticket work~\cite{frankle2019lottery} and the survey of Blalock et al.~\cite{blalock2020state}, which documents weak baselines and inconsistent comparisons across pruning papers, point the same way. We found no study that compares channel scores on classes held out from the ones used to choose the channels; that gap, with paired test episodes over several class partitions, is what this paper addresses.

\paragraph{Few-shot classification.}
Prototypical networks~\cite{snell2017proto} label a query by the nearest class mean in embedding space, and MAML~\cite{finn2017maml} learns an initialisation that adapts in a few gradient steps. Two findings shape our protocol. A network pre-trained with plain cross-entropy on the base classes is a strong baseline~\cite{chen2019closer,tian2020rethinking}, and episodic training on top of it with a cosine nearest-centroid head can add a little more~\cite{chen2021metabaseline}. Our stages S0 and M follow that recipe. The cross-domain benchmark of Guo et al.~\cite{guo2020bscd} has crop-disease and EuroSAT targets and reports that meta-learning methods often lose to simple fine-tuning when the target is far from the source. Strong pretrained backbones with a simple head~\cite{dhillon2020baseline,hu2022pushing} are the stronger reference point for any compact few-shot model. Section~\ref{sec:results} asks whether that holds when the episodic stage is added to a pruned network.

\paragraph{Earth observation.}
Few-shot scene classification in remote sensing has been approached with metric learning on aerial benchmarks~\cite{li2021rsmetanet} and with meta-learning for land cover from Sentinel-2 time series~\cite{russwurm2020meta}. NWPU-RESISC45~\cite{cheng2017resisc} and the Sentinel-2~\cite{drusch2012sentinel2} patches of EuroSAT~\cite{helber2019eurosat} are standard scene and land-use benchmarks, which we split by class instead of by image. Pretraining on satellite data~\cite{cong2022satmae} is the natural alternative to an ImageNet initialisation; we did not test it. On-board inference is not hypothetical: CloudScout~\cite{giuffrida2020cloudscout} runs a compact CNN on a Myriad~2 accelerator in the $\Phi$-Sat-1 mission~\cite{giuffrida2022phisat}. We do not target that hardware. Our question is narrower: when a ResNet-18 must shrink and must still recognise classes it was not trained on, does the choice of channels matter?

\paragraph{Plants.}
PlantVillage~\cite{hughes2015plantvillage} photographs single leaves against a plain background. PlantDoc~\cite{singh2020plantdoc} was assembled from internet photographs because large non-laboratory plant datasets were missing, and Barbedo~\cite{barbedo2018factors} lists image background and capture conditions among the factors that limit deep-learning disease recognition. Argüeso et al.~\cite{argueso2020fewshot} study few-shot recognition on PlantVillage with a $32/6$ class split and a Siamese network; we use a similar cross-class split with prototype classification. The method we test was first proposed for this setting, and crop monitoring is an Earth-observation task in its own right, but leaf photographs carry no geospatial context, so they are secondary here.

\paragraph{Earlier version of this method.}
A preprint~\cite{uddin2026preprint} introduced the names PMP and DACIS and reported results from an earlier implementation. This paper re-implements both from scratch, changes the protocol (class-disjoint splits, shared depth profile, paired episodes, five partitions), and reuses none of its numbers.
